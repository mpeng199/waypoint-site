WAYPOINT
Student Health Corps
Founding Cohort Onboarding
First training session · For the first group of volunteers
This is the first Waypoint training session. You're building something new, and this group is the founding cohort. They're excited but probably nervous. The goal: leave them confident in their role, clear on the guardrails, and ready to show up.

Timing: This outline runs about 90 minutes with room to breathe. Don't rush through.
Format: Conversational. You're a mentor, not a lecturer.
Group: High schoolers, mostly 14 to 18. Keep it real and practical.

1. Welcome and why this matters (10 minutes)

Start by acknowledging this is new. You're all figuring it out together.

  • Quick round: why did each person join? Keep it to one sentence each.
  • Tell them the one-sentence mission: you're a trained student volunteer corps that brings underserved New Yorkers to free health resources and professionals who already exist but go unused. In other words, you're the bridge between people and help that's already there.
  • Name the gap plainly: the help exists. What's missing is someone reliable to walk people to it. That's you.
  • End with: "You are the bridge, not the service." This frames everything that comes next.

Tone tip: this isn't a speech. Sit with them. Make eye contact. They're nervous; reassurance starts here.

2. How Waypoint works (15 minutes)

Explain what Waypoint actually does right now. There is one track, and this is it.

  • Resource Navigation (the whole job, right now): you show up at a library or senior center. Community members walk in. You help them find the free programs that exist, and you walk them to the certified counselor. That's it.
  • At every event, a certified counselor on site handles the regulated stuff (Medicare, benefits, insurance). Your job is information and referral only. You're not the expert.

Have the group read this aloud together, then sit with it for a minute:

"We are trained student volunteers who connect you to free and low-cost health resources that already exist in NYC. We are not doctors, nurses, social workers, or benefits counselors. We share information; we do not give medical or legal advice. We never charge for anything."

Ask: why does each sentence matter? Let them think. They'll get it: legal safety, managing expectations, trust.

3. The six ground rules (20 minutes)

These aren't arbitrary. They keep everyone (volunteers, residents, the program) safe and legal. Present each one, explain why it matters, then move on.

  • No enrollment or advice. Never help fill out a health insurance application or give medical or legal advice. Inform and refer only; the certified counselor handles enrollment.
  • No medical judgment. Don't ask about diagnosis, medical history, or help with medication. That's not your lane.
  • No one-on-one alone time. Never be alone with a vulnerable adult. Always work in pairs or groups with an adult supervisor present.
  • No collecting health info. Don't collect names, health details, or personal info. Log only anonymous counts, like "5 people asked about Medicare."
  • Immigration questions. If someone asks about immigration or public-charge status, don't guess. Route them to an immigration lawyer or advocate.
  • Nothing for Waypoint without approval. Don't post about Waypoint, make promises, or agree to partnerships without checking with me first. You represent the program.

If you're ever unsure: talk to your team lead or me. You're not expected to make hard calls alone. Escalating is always fine.

4. Walking through a real event (35 minutes)

This is where it gets real. Walk through what an event actually looks like, then practice it.

The event flow

  • Setup (5 min): arrive early, set up the table, make sure the certified counselor is there and knows the plan.
  • Greeting (10 min): welcome people as they come in. Be warm. Spend 30 seconds explaining: "Hi, I'm a student volunteer with Waypoint. We help connect people to free health and social programs in NYC. Do you have any questions about benefits, clinics, or programs?"
  • Listen and triage (15 min): ask what they need, then point to the matching resource in the directory. Keep it simple: "Looking for help with Medicare? Here's the number. Looking for a free clinic near you? Here's where to go."
  • Warm handoff (10 min): if they need enrollment help or insurance advice, introduce them to the counselor on site. If not, give them the number or address and wish them well.
  • Wrap up (5 min): log an anonymous count before the next person: "1 person asked about Medicare." That's it.

Your roles (rotate through them)

  • Greeter: welcome people, explain Waypoint in one sentence.
  • Triage: listen for their need, point to the resource. Translate if needed.
  • Handoff: introduce them to the certified counselor or give them the contact info.
  • Data logger: keep count of who came and what they asked about.

Practice: quick role-plays (15 minutes)

Run two or three scenarios and switch roles each time. The goal is getting comfortable with the opening line and the referral language, not memorizing a script.

  • They're overwhelmed. "I have no idea where to start. There's so much I need help with." Slow down, ask what's most urgent today, offer one next step, not everything at once.
  • They ask for enrollment help. "Can you help me sign up for Medicare?" Don't do it. Say warmly: "I can't fill that out, but the counselor here can help. Let me introduce you."
  • Immigration comes up. Someone mentions worrying about immigration status while asking about benefits. Don't advise. Say: "I hear you, that's an important question. Let me connect you with someone who specializes in this."
  • They ask if you're a nurse or doctor. "Are you a nurse or something?" Be direct and warm: "No, I'm a student volunteer. I can help you find the right free resource, but for medical stuff, I'll connect you with the professional here."

Facilitator note: if role-plays feel awkward, that's normal. It's their first time. The point is familiarity, not perfection. Keep it light.

5. Logistics and next steps (10 minutes)

  • Commitment: you're signing up for the semester, not just one event. Consistency matters to the people we serve.
  • Forms: you'll fill out a simple form (parental consent if you're under 18, code of conduct). Do this before you're scheduled for an event.
  • How we stay in touch: email or group chat. I'll send schedules, updates, questions.
  • What to bring: comfortable clothes, water, maybe a notepad.
  • If something feels off: text or talk to me. You're not expected to handle everything alone. I'm here to support you.
  • Your first event: [date/location TBD]. I'll confirm with each of you before you leave today.

Before you go

You just signed up for something meaningful. These are real people with real needs, and you're going to help connect them to resources that change their lives. That's no small thing.

You're going to make mistakes. That's okay. I'll make them too. We learn together.

Questions? Ask now, or ask me anytime: before, during, or after your first event.
