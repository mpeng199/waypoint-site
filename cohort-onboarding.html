WAYPOINT
Student Health Corps
Founding Cohort Onboarding
First training session · For the first group of volunteers
This is the first Waypoint training session. You're building something new, and this group is the founding cohort. They're excited but probably nervous. The goal: leave them confident in their role, clear on the guardrails, and ready to show up.

Timing: This outline runs about 90 minutes with room to breathe. Don't rush through.
Format: Conversational. You're a mentor, not a lecturer.
Group: High schoolers, mostly 14 to 18. Keep it real and practical.

1. Welcome and why this matters (10 minutes)

Start by acknowledging this is new. You're all figuring it out together.

  • Quick round: why did each person join? Keep it to one sentence each.
  • Tell them the one-sentence mission: we help New Yorkers buried in medical bills or fighting a denied claim reach the free experts who can actually help.
  • Name the gap plainly: hospitals here are required to offer financial assistance to patients under certain incomes, the state runs an independent appeal for denied claims, and free counselors take coverage disputes in dozens of languages. Almost nobody who qualifies ever hears about any of it. The help is real, it is free, and it is invisible.
  • End with: "You are the bridge, not the service." This frames everything that comes next.

Tone tip: this isn't a speech. Sit with them. Make eye contact. They're nervous; reassurance starts here.

2. How Waypoint works (15 minutes)

Explain what Waypoint actually does right now. There is one focus, and this is it: medical bills and coverage denials. Three activities, in order of how much time they should take.

  • Awareness and distribution (about 60% of the work). Getting accurate materials into libraries, senior centers, faith spaces, clinics, and community organisations. Most people who qualify for hospital charity care never apply because nobody told them it exists. This is the highest-impact, lowest-risk thing we do, and it requires no interpretation of anyone's situation.
  • Triage and routing (about 30%). Someone describes a problem in general terms. You identify which door is the right one and explain what that organisation does. Not whether they qualify. Which door.
  • Warm handoff (about 10%). Making the connection actually happen: dialling the helpline together, sitting with someone while they start a form, introducing them to a professional present at an event. A handed-over flyer is not a handoff.

General resource navigation — food, transit, senior services, benefits — is our second track. It is not cancelled, it is sequenced behind this one, and we do not do "just a little" of it at a billing event. Scope creep is how a narrowed mission blurs straight back.

Have the group read this aloud together, then sit with it for a minute:

We are trained student volunteers. We help you find the free programs and professionals in New York that handle medical bills and insurance denials. We are not doctors, lawyers, benefits counselors, or insurance experts. We do not read your bills, fill out your forms, or tell you what you qualify for. We connect you to people who do that, and they do it for free. We never charge for anything.

Ask: why does each sentence matter? Let them think. They'll get it: legal safety, managing expectations, trust.

3. The eight nevers (25 minutes)

These aren't arbitrary. They keep everyone (volunteers, residents, the programme) safe and legal. Present each one, explain why it matters, then move on. A volunteer must never:

  • Read, interpret, or comment on a denial letter, an explanation of benefits, an itemised bill, or any insurance document. Not even to help someone understand what it says.
  • Draft, edit, or help write an appeal letter, a financial assistance application, or any other submission.
  • Tell someone whether they qualify for anything. Not charity care, not a prescription programme, not an appeal.
  • State a deadline. See the box below.
  • Take custody of documents. No paper, no photographs, no emailed attachments, no "send it to me and I'll look."
  • Write down a name together with medical or financial details. Aggregate counts only.
  • Determine what kind of insurance plan someone has. Plan type decides which appeal route exists, and getting it wrong sends someone to a door that is not open to them. The counselors sort that out, not us.
  • Predict or promise an outcome. Not "they'll probably forgive it," not "you should win this."

Why we never quote a deadline: while we were building these materials, three official New York sources gave three different answers for the same appeal filing window. It does not matter which is currently right. If the state's own websites disagree, a sixteen-year-old quoting a number from memory will sometimes be wrong, and being wrong here can cost someone their appeal entirely. The professional confirms the deadline. You create urgency without numbers: "This is time-sensitive. Call them today, not next week."

The standing rules from day one still apply on top of these:

  • No one-on-one alone time. Never be alone with a vulnerable adult. Always work in pairs or groups with an adult supervisor present.
  • Immigration questions. If someone asks about immigration or public-charge status, don't guess. Route them to an immigration lawyer or advocate. (A hospital cannot consider immigration status when it looks at financial assistance — that much you can say.)
  • No medical judgment. Don't ask about diagnosis, medical history, or medication. That's not your lane.
  • Nothing for Waypoint without approval. Don't post about Waypoint, make promises, or agree to partnerships without checking with me first.

If you're ever unsure: talk to your team lead or me. Escalation goes volunteer, then team lead, then me, then the professional partner. You're not expected to make hard calls alone. Escalating is always correct and never counts against anyone.

4. What we say instead (20 minutes)

The nevers are only useful if you have a replacement sentence ready. Practise these out loud until they sound like you.

  • "Can you look at this letter?" → "I'm not able to read your documents, but the people at this number do that all day and it's free. Want to call them now?"
  • "Do you think I qualify?" → "I can't tell you that, and I'd hate to guess wrong. They can tell you in one phone call."
  • "How long do I have?" → "It's time-sensitive, so I wouldn't wait. Call today and they'll tell you exactly."
  • "Will they cancel my bill?" → "I don't know, and I don't want to promise. What I do know is that a lot of people who qualify never apply, so it's worth finding out."
  • "Are you a lawyer or a social worker?" → "No, I'm a student volunteer. My job is getting you to the person who can actually help."
  • "Can you call for me?" → "I can dial and sit with you while you talk to them." (This one is allowed, and it's the strongest version of a handoff.)

5. Walking through a real event (30 minutes)

This is where it gets real. Walk through what an event actually looks like, then practise it.

The event flow

  • Setup (5 min): arrive early, set up the table, make sure the professional is there and knows the plan. An event without a certified professional present is not a Waypoint event.
  • Greeting (10 min): welcome people as they come in. Be warm. Spend 30 seconds explaining: "Hi, I'm a student volunteer with Waypoint. We help people find free help with medical bills and insurance denials. Is that something you're dealing with, or anyone you know?"
  • Listen and route (15 min): ask them to describe the problem in their own words, then name the door. Keep it simple: "That sounds like something the free counselors handle. Here's the number, and I can dial it with you right now."
  • Warm handoff (10 min): dial together, or walk them to the professional at the next table. Don't hand over a flyer and call it done.
  • Wrap up (5 min): log an anonymous count before the next person: "one person asked about a hospital bill." That's it. No names, ever.

Your roles (rotate through them)

  • Greeter: welcome people, explain Waypoint in one sentence.
  • Routing: listen for the problem, name the matching door. Translate if needed.
  • Handoff: dial the helpline and stay in the chair, or introduce them to the professional on site.
  • Data logger: keep anonymous counts of how many people came and which door each was routed to.

Practice: quick role-plays (15 minutes)

Run three or four scenarios and switch roles each time. The goal is getting comfortable with the opening line and the referral language, not memorizing a script.

  • They put a bill on the table. "Can you look at this and tell me what it means?" Don't take it or read it. Say: "I'm not able to read your documents, but the people at this number do exactly that, for free. Can I dial it with you?"
  • They ask if you can get it lowered. "Can you get this reduced?" Be honest and warm: "I don't know, and I don't want to promise. What I do know is that a lot of people who qualify never apply, so it's worth finding out from someone who can actually tell you."
  • They're overwhelmed. "I have no idea where to start. There's so much I need help with." Slow down, ask what's most urgent today, offer one next step, not everything at once.
  • Immigration comes up. Someone mentions worrying about immigration status. Don't advise. Say: "I hear you, that's an important question. Let me connect you with someone who specializes in this."
  • They ask if you're a nurse or a lawyer. "Are you a lawyer or something?" Be direct and warm: "No, I'm a student volunteer. My job is getting you to the person who can actually help."

Facilitator note: if role-plays feel awkward, that's normal. It's their first time. The point is familiarity, not perfection. Keep it light.

6. Logistics and next steps (10 minutes)

  • Commitment: you're signing up for the semester, not just one event. Consistency matters to the people we serve.
  • Forms: you'll fill out a simple form (parental consent if you're under 18, code of conduct). Do this before you're scheduled for an event.
  • How we stay in touch: email or group chat. I'll send schedules, updates, questions.
  • What to bring: comfortable clothes, water, maybe a notepad.
  • If something feels off: text or talk to me. You're not expected to handle everything alone. I'm here to support you.
  • Your first event: [date/location TBD]. I'll confirm with each of you before you leave today.

Before you go

You just signed up for something meaningful. These are real people with real needs, and you're going to help connect them to resources that change their lives. That's no small thing.

You're going to make mistakes. That's okay. I'll make them too. We learn together.

Questions? Ask now, or ask me anytime: before, during, or after your first event.
