What the job actually is.

The whole role, written down before you decide: the three things you do, the eight things you never do, and the sentence to reach for when someone asks you something you are not allowed to answer.

Sign up →
The short version. You are the bridge, not the service. You put accurate materials where people already are, you listen to someone describe a problem, you name the organization that handles that kind of problem, and you dial the number and stay in the chair while it rings. You never read anyone's paperwork, never say who qualifies, and never quote a deadline.

The three things you do

Getting materials out is most of it. Accurate flyers and referral cards into libraries, senior centers, faith spaces, clinics and community organizations. Most people who qualify for hospital charity care never apply, and the reason is almost always that nobody told them it existed. This is the part that reaches the most people, and it asks you to interpret nobody's situation.

Then routing. Someone describes a problem in their own words. You work out which door is the right one and explain what that organization does. Not whether they qualify. Which door.

Then the handoff, which is the smallest part and the one that matters most. Dialing the helpline together, sitting with someone while they start a form, walking them over to a professional who is there at the event. A flyer handed over is not a handoff.

Food, transit, senior services and benefits are the other half of what Waypoint does. Not at a billing event, though. One focus per table.

The eight nevers

These are not arbitrary, and they are not negotiable. They keep you, the person in front of you, and the whole program safe. A volunteer never:

  • Reads, interprets or comments on a denial letter, an explanation of benefits, an itemized bill or any insurance document. Not even to help someone understand what it says.
  • Drafts, edits or helps write an appeal letter, a financial assistance application, or any other submission.
  • Tells someone whether they qualify for anything. Not charity care, not a prescription program, not an appeal.
  • States a deadline. Three official New York sources gave us three different answers for the same appeal window. If the state's own websites disagree, a sixteen-year-old quoting a number from memory will sometimes be wrong, and being wrong here can cost someone their appeal.
  • Takes custody of documents. No paper, no photographs, no emailed attachments, no "send it to me and I'll look."
  • Writes down a name next to a medical or financial detail. Anonymous counts only.
  • Works out what kind of insurance plan someone has. Plan type decides which appeal route exists, and getting it wrong sends someone to a door that is not open to them.
  • Predicts or promises an outcome. Not "they'll probably forgive it." Not "you should win this."

On top of those: never be alone with a vulnerable adult, always work in pairs or groups with an adult there, send anything touching immigration law to a lawyer or advocate rather than guessing, and ask before you post about Waypoint or agree to anything on its behalf.

If you are unsure, escalate. It goes volunteer, then team lead, then the founder, then the professional partner. Nobody expects you to make a hard call alone, and escalating has never counted against anyone.

What you say instead

A rule you cannot replace with a sentence is a rule you will break under pressure. These are the replacements. Say them out loud until they sound like you.

  • They ask, "Can you look at this letter?" You say: "I'm not able to read your documents, but the people at this number do that all day and it's free. Want to call them now?"
  • They ask, "Do you think I qualify?" You say: "I can't tell you that, and I'd hate to guess wrong. They can tell you in one phone call."
  • They ask, "How long do I have?" You say: "It's time-sensitive, so I wouldn't wait. Call today and they'll tell you exactly."
  • They ask, "Will they cancel my bill?" You say: "I don't know, and I don't want to promise. What I do know is that a lot of people who qualify never apply, so it's worth finding out."
  • They ask, "Are you a lawyer or a social worker?" You say: "No, I'm a student volunteer. My job is getting you to the person who can actually help."
  • They ask, "Can you call for me?" You say: "I can dial and sit with you while you talk to them." That one is allowed, and it is the strongest version of a handoff.

What an event looks like

You arrive early and help set up the table. An event without a certified professional there is not a Waypoint event, so you check that first.

Then you greet people as they come past. Thirty seconds: "Hi, I'm a student volunteer with Waypoint. We help people find free help with medical bills and insurance denials. Is that something you're dealing with, or anyone you know?"

When someone says yes, you ask them to describe it in their own words and you name the door. "That sounds like something the free counselors handle. Here's the number, and I can dial it with you right now." Then you dial, or you walk them to the professional at the next table.

Before the next person, you log one anonymous count: one person asked about a hospital bill. That is the whole record. No names, ever.

You will rotate through the roles rather than pick one: greeting, routing, handoff, and keeping the counts.

What it asks of you

One training session before you speak to anyone, about ninety minutes, conversational rather than a lecture. It covers the routing, the nevers and the replacement sentences, and you practice the awkward conversations with each other before you have them with a stranger.

After that, events. You work in pairs or groups, never alone, with an adult present. Most volunteers are between fourteen and eighteen.

No experience is needed, and no medical or legal knowledge is wanted. The whole design of the role is that you are not the expert and never pretend to be. If you speak a language other than English, say so on the form. A conversation about a frightening bill rarely survives a stranger translating it.

And nothing starts until the materials are checked. No volunteer speaks to anyone about a bill until someone with real health-insurance-law knowledge has been over what we hand out.

The statement you will say out loud

This is printed on everything we hand out and said at every table. It is the sentence that stops someone mistaking you for a professional, and it does not get reworded.

We are trained student volunteers. We help you find the free programs and professionals in New York that handle medical bills and insurance denials. We are not doctors, lawyers, benefits counselors, or insurance experts. We do not read your bills, fill out your forms, or tell you what you qualify for. We connect you to people who do that, and they do it for free. We never charge for anything.
