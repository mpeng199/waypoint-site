It starts with a letter nobody wants to open.

Written in a language built for somebody else.

Stripped down it says: they said no, and this is yours to pay.

Every line of it has a plain meaning. The last one is a door.

So we carry it to people.

We find New Yorkers stuck with a medical bill or a denied claim, and we walk them to the free experts who already handle exactly that.

Where the thread leads

There is a solution for this. It already has a name.

Every hospital in New York keeps a financial assistance policy, and its own financial counselor is the one who applies it. We hand over that number.

When an insurer denies a claim twice, the state can send the file to a reviewer who does not work for them. That decision binds the insurer.

New York funds counselors who take coverage problems by phone, in dozens of languages, free to whoever calls. They argue with insurers for a living.

State programmes for older New Yorkers, and the drug makers' own schemes, sit behind the counter unmentioned. A pharmacist is the one who checks.

The statement in full

We are trained student volunteers. We help you find the free programs and professionals in New York that handle medical bills and insurance denials. We are not doctors, lawyers, benefits counselors, or insurance experts. We do not read your bills, fill out your forms, or tell you what you qualify for. We connect you to people who do that, and they do it for free. We never charge for anything.

Printed on everything we hand out, and said out loud at every table.

Trained to know exactly where the line is.

Listens, in your language. You describe what you are holding, in your own words. A volunteer never reads it back to you or tells you what it means.

Names the office that handles it. They can tell you who takes this kind of problem. They can never tell you whether you qualify for it.

Dials the number and stays while it rings. You do the talking. A volunteer sits with you through the wait and never speaks in your place.

Leaves with only your contact information. No photo of your paperwork, no copy of your bill, and no collection of sensitive information of any kind.

And none of it starts until it is checked. No volunteer speaks to anyone about a bill until someone with real health-insurance-law knowledge has been over our materials.

It was never only about the bill.

Walking people to the free health resources they are missing — food, transit, senior services, benefits — is where Waypoint started, and it is still half of why we exist.

For partners

The people you have been trying to reach all year.

Our students speak the languages your block speaks. A conversation about a frightening bill rarely survives a stranger translating it, and almost never survives a leaflet.

Nobody is asked for a status. A hospital cannot weigh immigration status when it looks at financial assistance, and anything touching immigration law goes to a professional rather than a student's best guess.

And you get counts you can use. Every quarter: how many we sent you and what they asked about. Categories, never names.

Not money. Not an endorsement. Your materials.

If you already do this work, we would like to carry it. Flyers, referral cards, or a QR code we can put in front of people. We cover the printing and we do the distribution.

And a name we can route to, so a referral is a handoff instead of a hope. In return: distribution you don't have to staff, and a quarterly count.

Twenty minutes of reading, if you can spare it. A look over our routing sheet, so we know we are not overstating anything. We would far rather be corrected by you now than be wrong in front of somebody who needed us to be right.

And we have not done this yet. Waypoint has not held its first event. Whoever says yes first shapes how the whole thing runs, and is named in the first semester report.
