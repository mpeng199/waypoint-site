Real. Free. And invisible.

Hospitals in New York are required to offer financial assistance to patients under certain incomes. The state runs an independent appeal when an insurer refuses to pay. Free counselors handle coverage disputes in dozens of languages. None of that is written on the letter in somebody's hand.

So we carry it to people.

Waypoint is a trained student volunteer corps. We find New Yorkers buried in medical bills or fighting a denied claim, and we get them to the free professionals who already handle exactly that. We are not building a second system. We are the introduction the first one never makes.

The envelope nobody wanted to open.

A bill for more than a month's rent. A letter saying the insurer will not pay for something that already happened. A prescription quietly stopped because the copay moved. Something somebody has been carrying for weeks.

Hospitals here are required to offer financial assistance to patients under certain incomes. The state runs an independent review when a plan says no and will not change its mind. Free counselors take coverage disputes in dozens of languages, and national nonprofits will file a charity care application on somebody's behalf at no cost at all.

Every one of them arrives alone, with nothing on it about where to go next.

All of that already exists. None of it is printed on the side of the page that arrives.

Almost nobody who qualifies ever uses any of it. Not because they were turned away. Because no one told them it was there. The help is real, it is free, and it is invisible — which makes this a distribution problem rather than a funding one, and distribution is a thing a group of students can genuinely fix.

So most of what we do is carrying it to where people already are. It is the plainest work we do, it is the highest leverage, and it requires no interpretation of anyone's situation whatsoever.

And when somebody describes a problem, we name the door. Not whether they qualify, which is never ours to say. Which organisation handles this, what it does, and what it costs, which is nothing. When we are not certain, we route to the free statewide counselors whose job is exactly this, because guessing at a more specific door and getting it wrong costs someone time they may not have.

What a referral looks like when it works.

“They sent me this months ago. I can't pay it and I don't know what half of it is for.”

A volunteer listens, in whatever language that conversation works best in. They do not ask to see the paperwork, and they do not need to.

“Somebody told me there's nothing you can do once they say no.”

The student names the organisation that handles exactly this, and says what it does. Not whether it will work. Not who qualifies. Never a filing date recalled from memory.

“So who do I call?”

They dial it together and stay in the chair while it rings. Or they start the form on the spot. Or they walk somebody to the professional already sitting at the next table.

Nobody leaves holding a pamphlet. They leave with one thing they can act on — a name, a number a person actually answers, a call already placed — and with the knowledge that it cost them nothing and will keep costing nothing.

Every student is vetted before they meet anyone. Onboarding, parental consent, a background check where the setting calls for one, and whatever additional training you require of your own volunteers. Your rules layered on top of ours, never instead of them. They commit by the semester rather than by the afternoon, so no shift depends on one teenager's week going well.

The rule about dates has a reason. While we were building our routing materials, three official New York sources gave three different answers for the same appeal filing window. If the state's own pages disagree, a sixteen-year-old quoting a number from memory will sometimes be wrong, and being wrong there can cost somebody their appeal entirely. So the volunteer creates urgency without numbers, and the professional confirms the date.

And no volunteer speaks to anybody about a bill until this has been checked by someone who would know. Our routing sheet and volunteer scripts go to a reviewer with real health-insurance-law knowledge, to confirm that nothing in them states eligibility, quotes a deadline, promises an outcome, or reads like legal advice. Handing out reviewed materials is fine before that. Conversations about somebody's actual situation are not.

The rest of it is not forgotten.

Connecting underserved New Yorkers to any free health resource they are missing — senior centers, benefits counselors, food programs, transit fares — is where Waypoint started and is still half of why it exists. It is our second track, and it is sequenced deliberately behind this one. We open it when we have partners confirmed, a cohort actually showing up, distribution runs completed and logged, a second lead who owns the billing work, and a written report. Running two half-built programmes would cost the people we serve far more than waiting one semester will.

Our students already speak it. Multilingual volunteers are the point of this, not a bonus feature — a conversation about a bill somebody is frightened of rarely survives being translated by a stranger, and it almost never survives a leaflet.

Nobody is asked for a status. A hospital cannot consider immigration status when it looks at financial assistance, and the counselors we route to help people regardless of it. Anything that actually touches immigration law goes to an immigration professional rather than a student's best guess, because wrong information there costs a family far more than the appointment was ever worth.

Behind every referral is a directory we maintain. A curated set of the organisations that handle bills, denials, prior authorisation, prescription costs, and the free services around them, each entry carrying the date it was last checked and a named human on the other end wherever one exists. It is re-verified every semester by someone whose job that is. A directory that sends an eighty-year-old to a disconnected number is worse than no directory at all.

And you get counts you can actually use. Every quarter we tell you how many people we sent your way and what they were asking about, broken out by the kind of problem rather than by the person. Categories, never names, no medical details, nothing that could identify anyone — and exactly the shape of thing your board or your funder asks you for in the spring.

Not money. Not an endorsement. Your materials.

If you already do this work, we would like to carry it. Flyers, referral cards, or a QR code we can put in front of people at libraries, senior centers, faith spaces, and community events. We cover the printing and we do the distribution. We are not asking for money, for an endorsement, or for staff time beyond one conversation.

And a name we can route to. One person or one line we send people toward, so that a referral is a handoff instead of a hope. In return you get distribution you do not have to staff, and a quarterly count of how many people we sent your way and what they were asking about.

Twenty minutes of reading, if you can spare it. A look over the one-page routing sheet our volunteers work from, so we know we are not overstating anything. We would far rather be corrected by you now than be wrong in front of somebody who needed us to be right. It is a read and a reply, not a supervision commitment.

If what you have is a room, that is the other ask. A library branch, a senior center, a community or faith hall. Shelf space for materials, or a small table at something you were already running. We bring the volunteers and the materials, we work inside your supervision and your rules, and any community event we take part in is co-staffed by a certified professional who handles anything regulated.

And we have not done this yet.

Waypoint has not held its first event, and no volunteer will speak to anybody about a bill until our materials clear that review. Whoever says yes first shapes how the whole thing runs, is named in the first semester report, and is the reason there is a second one.
